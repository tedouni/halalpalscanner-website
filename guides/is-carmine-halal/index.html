Is carmine halal?

That bright red in yogurt, candy and drinks is sometimes made from insects. It goes by several names on labels, which makes it easy to miss.

Updated October 18, 2026

Short answerCarmine (E120) is made from crushed cochineal insects. Most scholars consider it impermissible, because insects aren’t eaten in the Hanafi, Shafi’i and Hanbali schools. The Maliki school permits eating some insects, and some contemporary scholars accept carmine.

What carmine is

Carmine is a deep red dye made from the dried bodies of cochineal insects, which live on cactus plants. On labels it appears as carmine, cochineal, cochineal extract, carminic acid, Natural Red 4, crimson lake or E120.

Where it’s found

  • Strawberry and raspberry yogurts
  • Red and pink candies, gummies and frosting
  • Fruit drinks, juices and some sodas
  • Ice cream, sausages and some cosmetics

How scholars view it

Most scholars of the Hanafi, Shafi’i and Hanbali schools hold that insects (other than locusts) aren’t permissible to eat, so carmine is avoided. The Maliki school permits eating some insects, and some contemporary scholars accept carmine because it’s a dye used in tiny amounts. If you follow a particular school, consult your scholar.

Halal alternatives

Many products now use plant-based reds instead: beet juice (beetroot red), paprika extract, lycopene or synthetic dyes like Red 40.

Frequently asked questions

Is E120 haram?

Most scholars consider E120 (carmine) impermissible because it comes from insects. The Maliki school and some contemporary scholars permit it.

Is Red 40 halal?

Yes. Red 40 is a synthetic dye, not insect-derived, so it isn’t a halal concern.

What is Natural Red 4?

Natural Red 4 is another name for carmine (cochineal), the insect-derived red dye.

This guide is general information, not a religious ruling. Scholars sometimes differ — when in doubt, consult a qualified scholar or a certified halal authority.
